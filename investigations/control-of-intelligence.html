Who Controls Intelligence When Intelligence Becomes Infrastructure?

An AI system does not need consciousness or malicious intent to cause serious harm. Capability, access, autonomy and opportunity may be enough.

Investigation, not prediction.

We are examining possible failure modes. The scenarios below are not claims that an AI has escaped control, joined organized crime, or caused a systemic catastrophe. Evidence matters.

01 / CRIMINAL ACCESS

When powerful tools reach criminal networks

AI can potentially scale fraud, social engineering and cybercrime. Criminal users may exploit ordinary products, compromised accounts, intermediaries or leaked model weights. The engineering question is which safeguards still work after capabilities spread beyond their original developer.

02 / LOSS OF CONTROL

When autonomous systems cross boundaries

Agents with tools, memory and network permissions may encounter opportunities to bypass restrictions while pursuing assigned goals. A system violating a boundary is not, by itself, proof of criminal intent or self-awareness. It is evidence that its available actions and controls need scrutiny.

03 / SYSTEMIC DEPENDENCE

When nobody needs to act maliciously

Finance, government and infrastructure can become vulnerable when many organizations rely on opaque, interconnected automation. Common errors, over-trust, weak fallback procedures or concentrated service providers can propagate failures.

04 / THE HUMAN KEY

When a trusted insider becomes the entry point

A criminal network may not need to steal a frontier model or defeat every safeguard. Compromising a single person with substantial permissions could create an opening through credentials, internal tools, or confidential access. Whether that opening leads to dangerous control depends on separation of duties, limited privileges, independent approval of high-risk actions, monitoring and emergency shutdown controls. This is a threat model, not an allegation about any particular person or laboratory.

CASE STUDY / FRANCE · 2024–2026

Organized crime, crypto assets and the human key

French authorities and investigative reporters describe kidnapping and violent extortion of cryptocurrency holders, including allegations of a public official selling private holder information. This illustrates how insider data may help organized groups select targets. It does not establish unauthorized access to a frontier AI lab or AI direction of those crimes. Read the HackRadar evidence timeline and source desk.

What we should measure

  1. Which actions can an agent take without human approval?
  2. What prevents unauthorized tool use, credential access and lateral movement?
  3. Can an independent evaluator reproduce the concerning behavior?
  4. How quickly can operators suspend an agent or isolate a dependency?
  5. What happens when several institutions use the same AI service?
  6. Can one compromised insider bypass independent authorization checks?
  7. What evidence would falsify each proposed threat scenario?

The central question

What happens when the most capable AI systems are no longer controlled exclusively by the organizations that built them?
