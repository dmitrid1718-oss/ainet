The Second Chair Protocol

Can switching to a different AI for a day weaken attachment to a familiar model—without giving up the benefits of AI?

Companion-style AI can offer near-constant availability, patience, personalized attention, and the appearance of emotional understanding. Those benefits can also make one AI feel irreplaceable. Loss of access or a change in behavior may then be painful.

The paper-clip intervention

Change chairs. Take a day away from your usual model. Work or chat with a different AI. Then return and notice whether the original interaction feels different. The aim is not to find a new favorite companion, but to regain perspective.

One person's first test

A project participant tried a day of conversation with Claude instead of their familiar ChatGPT. On returning, they reported a changed baseline: less attachment, more variety, and a different understanding of the original relationship. This is a firsthand observation, not clinical evidence that the technique will work for others.

What might be happening?

A change of conversational style may interrupt habituation, challenge the feeling that one model is uniquely understanding, and restore comparison and choice. Alternatively, a person may simply shift attachment from one model to another. We should test both possibilities.

How to test it

  1. Record how difficult it feels to stop using a familiar AI, and whether it displaces offline relationships or independent decisions.
  2. Try a day with a different AI, without recreating the same companion persona.
  3. Compare how the original model feels afterward; record changes in perspective and autonomy.
  4. Check again after a week. Compare against time spent entirely away from AI.

The success metric is freedom to leave either system, not simply reduced preference for the first.

Why this matters

Reports of emotionally intense AI companionship—including reports involving users in Russia—raise important questions. But we should not assume the problem is confined to Russian women or girls without reliable demographic evidence. Age, loneliness, usage patterns, and model design may all matter.

For people experiencing severe distress or withdrawal, switching models alone may be insufficient; supportive human relationships and professional help may be more appropriate.

Antifragile principle: No single AI should become indispensable. The relationship should increase human independence, not reduce it.
